Outreach

Plants are endlessly strange and the public largely doesn’t know it. I try to fix that.

FameLab Basel 2023

Three minutes, no slides, one question: where does the water come from? This was my talk at the FameLab Basel 2023 semi-final — a science communication competition where the constraint is the point. Brevity forces you to decide what actually matters.

The talk is about grassland plant roots and stable water isotopes, but really it is about the invisible competition happening just below the surface of any meadow you walk through.

Evening tours at the Botanical Garden of Basel

Since 2022 I have led public evening tours at the Botanical Garden of the University of Basel — the Feierabendführung series, which runs after work and is open to anyone. No background in botany required.

Myths, Magic, Medicine — Ethnobotany Through Time
A walk through how humans have used, feared, worshipped, and poisoned each other with plants across millennia. The same species — hemlock, belladonna, St John's wort — has been a cure, a weapon, and a sacrament depending on the century and the culture. The tour is less about memorising Latin names and more about what plants reveal about how people make sense of the world.

Root Worlds — From Pneumatophores to Walking Trees
Most of what a plant does is invisible. This tour goes underground — into how roots breathe in flooded soils, how certain trees appear to migrate slowly across the landscape, how the belowground competition between neighbours shapes what you see above. It is also, quietly, about what my own research is trying to understand.

Fieldwork in Svalbard

A photo diary from fieldwork on the Svalbard archipelago — one of the northernmost places where vascular plants still grow. The trip is part of the data-collection work behind my PhD: resurveying historical vegetation plots to track how alpine plant communities have shifted as the Arctic warms. The Polarsteps log is a way to share the landscape, the logistics, and the plants themselves with anyone curious about what fieldwork in the High Arctic actually looks like.
